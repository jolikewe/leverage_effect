Leverage effect in futures Source on GitHub

When prices fall, volatility rises — but only for equities.

Ten anonymised futures, 1982 to 2017. Five behave like equity indices and show a leverage effect that is large, significant and lasts a month. The five that look like government bonds mostly show none.

One effect, five questions

The leverage effect says that a price fall is followed by higher volatility than a price rise of the same size. The data set holds daily returns of ten futures labelled INST0 to INST9; what each one is was never given, so the study first works it out from behaviour alone.

  1. 1MeasurementWhich instruments show a leverage effect? This also reveals the equity indices.
  2. 2Significance and stabilityIs it distinguishable from chance, and does it hold in every calendar year?
  3. 3DirectionDo returns predict volatility, or does volatility predict returns?
  4. 4SpilloverDo equity moves change bond volatility, or the reverse?
  5. 5StrategyDoes a better volatility forecast make a volatility-targeted long book earn more?
Question 1

Measure it, and the equities identify themselves

A correlation with a time lag

Each return is divided by the instrument's trailing one-year volatility so that quiet and wild markets are comparable. Then L(τ) = cov(xt, x²t+τ) / ⟨x²⟩² asks: when today's return is lower, is the squared return τ days later higher?

For the five equity indices the answer is yes, and loudly. On the first day after the move, L(1) is −0.244 for INST5, −0.316 for INST7 and −0.272 for INST9.

Bonds barely register

Same formula, same scale, five other instruments. Four of them sit close to zero, with L(1) between −0.011 and −0.092. A government bond falling in price is just a yield rising; there is no forced selling or panic to feed volatility.

One of them, INST2, stands apart. Keep an eye on it.

Add up a month

Summing L(τ) over the next 22 trading days gives a single score, S. The lines now accumulate, and the two families pull apart: equities end between −1.9 and −3.1, four of the bonds between −0.2 and −0.45.

Fitting each curve to an exponential decay gives an amplitude near 0.18 to 0.32 and a decay time of 8 to 14 days for the equities.

INST2 behaves like an equity that forgets faster

Its S of −1.292 is the only bond-labelled score that is clearly significant, and its curve starts at −0.188. But the effect fades in about 6 days, where equities need 8 to 14.

It is still no equity: its yearly S is negative in only 62% of calendar years, as shown further down.

Question 2

Five clear of the line, five that are not

To test against chance, each series is rebuilt a thousand times with its magnitudes kept and its signs flipped at random. That destroys any link between direction and later volatility. The spread of S in those fakes turns the real S into a z-score, z = S / σnull. The leverage hypothesis is directional (S below zero), so the test is one-sided; with ten instruments tested, a Bonferroni threshold of p ≤ 0.005 (z below −2.58) keeps the false-alarm rate in check.

All five equity indices reject "no leverage effect": z between −5.0 and −6.3, and p at the permutation floor of 1/1001. INST2 also clears the line at z = −3.54. INST4 (one-sided p = 0.010) and INST0 (p = 0.026) do not clear the Bonferroni threshold, and the other two bonds are well short. A two-sided test, which would also catch bonds moving the other way, gives the same verdicts.

Stable across years, only where it should be

S is recomputed inside each calendar year, scaled by the full-sample variance so that calm years are not made to look extreme. Non-overlapping years keep the checks independent. Each column below is one year; amber means falls were followed by higher volatility.

The equity indices have S below zero in 89 to 100% of calendar years (INST5 and INST8 in every one). Each equity index passes an exact sign test (p ≤ 2×10⁻⁵). The bonds sit between 56 and 68%, close to a coin toss. Nothing trends: with only 16 to 34 years per instrument, no slope is significant under small-sample tests (HC3 t-test, permutation, Mann-Kendall; all p above 0.07).

Method in brief

Normalise

Returns are divided by the lagged 252-day rolling volatility, so every instrument sits on one scale and no lookahead enters.

Leverage correlation

L(τ) = cov(xt, x²t+τ) / ⟨x²⟩², summed over τ = 1 to 22 to give S. Significance comes from a one-sided test against 1000 sign-flip surrogates.

Direction

Next-5-day variance is regressed on daily, weekly and monthly realised variance (HAR) plus the return and its absolute value, with Newey-West errors. A reverse regression tests volatility to returns.

Strategy

Position equals a 10% target divided by forecast volatility, with a 20% rebalancing band, next-close trading and half-spread costs of 1bp for equities and 0.5bp for bonds. Trend votes use 1, 3 and 12-month lookbacks.

Comparison

Forecasts are scored by QLIKE and compared with a Diebold-Mariano test. Sharpe differences use a 21-day block bootstrap.

Key findings

1 and 2Equities carry it, bonds do not

All five equity indices reject the null (one-sided p at the 0.001 floor, below the Bonferroni 0.005) and have S below zero in 89 to 100% of calendar years. INST2 reacts like an equity but fades in about 6 days instead of 8 to 14; the other bonds show no stable effect.

3Returns lead volatility

For every equity index, the return coefficient on next-week variance is negative (gamma from −0.251 to −0.145, p below 1e-4) after controlling for past variance, with and without the 1987 and 2008 crisis windows. Past volatility does not predict returns, apart from INST9.

4A small spillover, one way

Equity falls raise next-week bond variance slightly (basket theta = −0.054, p = 0.018; modest evidence that does not survive a study-wide correction). Bond returns do not predict equity volatility (basket p = 0.56).

5Forecast accuracy is not P&L

HAR cuts QLIKE against trailing volatility (equities 0.518 to 0.397, bonds 0.400 to 0.342), and leverage terms help equities again (0.385). The Sharpe of the full book goes 0.916, 1.018, 0.990 and 1.044 across the four sizings, but none of those gaps is convincingly significant.
